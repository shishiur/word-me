# What is a common-pool resource? [Resource attributes versus property rights]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Question for this chapter:</b> when we call the fish of a haor a "commons", are we describing the resource or people's rights?</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> none. Keep a picture of a beel or river in mind.</div>
<div><b>Used in:</b> W02 (p.278–279), W05 (§2.1), W06 (p.132–133), W09 (Box 1), W10 (§5.2)</div>
<div><b>Time:</b> 40 minutes reading + 10 minutes writing</div>
</div>
:::
:::

Picture a haor in the monsoon. Fishers from ten surrounding villages cast their nets in the same water. If one of them catches more today, there is less for the others tomorrow. Keeping anyone out is also hard: you cannot fence such a large expanse, guarding costs money, and boats move at night. When these two attributes occur together, institutional scholars call the resource a **common-pool resource** (CPR).

Ostrom and colleagues wrote the definition with care. A CPR is a resource system in which (1) excluding beneficiaries through physical or institutional means is especially costly, and (2) exploitation by one user reduces availability for others <span class="ev D">D</span> (Ostrom et al. 1999, p.278). Notice that in the sentence just before, they insist that concepts about the "resource system" be separated from concepts about "property rights", and that the term CPR refers to resource systems "regardless of the property rights involved". That separation is the first lesson of this book.

::: {.box .concept}
<span class="label">Core concept</span>
**CPR names an attribute of a resource; common (group) property names a rights regime; open access means the absence of effective rights.** Treat the three as synonyms and the whole literature will be misread. Ostrom et al. (1999, Table 1) list four regimes: open access, group property, individual property and government property.
:::

### Why the concept was needed

In 1968 Garrett Hardin argued that on a pasture open to all, each herder would add one more animal for private gain, and the collective result would be ruin. His proposed remedy was "either socialism or the privatism of free enterprise" (quoted in W02, p.278). The trouble is that many applied his story to every "commons". W02 notes that this "starkness" was used to justify central government control <span class="ev D">D</span>. Separating CPR from property regime changes the question. Instead of asking "Will the resource be destroyed?", we ask: **under which rules and rights regimes, and in what circumstances,** do users build sustainable use?

Fisheries economists had modelled the problem more than a decade before Hardin. W10 (§5.2, p.62) recalls that Gordon (1954) and Scott (1955) showed how, without property rights, surplus labour and capital flow into a fishery, rents dissipate and stocks decline. The individual-quota (ITQ) solution later grew from this line of work. The argument "no rights, therefore ruin" thus has two lineages: Hardin's metaphor and fisheries-economics models.

The same haor fishery can sit under four different regimes. Conceptual sources: Ostrom et al. (1999) Table 1; Agrawal et al. (2023) §2.1. ::: fig-read
First decide which cell of the top grid your resource falls into (attributes). Then, in the bottom row, ask who can exclude whom at this moment and who makes the rules (rights). The two answers can differ.
:::

### Mechanism: how the two attributes create a dilemma

When exclusion is hard, anyone can enter; when use is subtractive, each person's harvest raises costs for everyone else. W02 (p.279) therefore describes two forms of free-riding: (a) overuse without regard for the harm to others, and (b) failure to contribute to maintaining or improving the resource <span class="ev D">D</span>. In a haor, the first is catching egg-bearing fish; the second is not giving labour to re-dig a beel's connecting channel.

W02 lists further attributes that change how hard governance is: size and carrying capacity, measurability, the spatial and temporal availability of flows, storage, whether the resource moves (water, wildlife, most fish) or stays put (trees), and the rate of regeneration <span class="ev D">D</span> (p.279). The authors note that counting trees in a forest is relatively easy, while assessing migratory fish stocks and irrigation water in a system without storage is far harder. For a hilsa researcher this is directly relevant: hilsa is a textbook example of a mobile, seasonal, hard-to-measure resource.

::: {.box .method}
<span class="label">Operationalising</span>
A field question for excludability: "How much cost and risk does it take to stop an outside boat entering your jalmohal?" Measuring subtractability needs biological data: does catch per unit effort (CPUE) fall as fishing pressure rises? The first is social-institutional data, the second ecological. This is where interdisciplinary work becomes necessary.
:::

### The attributes are not fixed

W05 adds an important subtlety. Excludability and subtractability are "characteristics of goods", but institutions and technologies determine how easy exclusion is. The invention of barbed wire, for example, made the privatisation of land in the American West possible <span class="ev D">D</span> (Agrawal et al. 2023, §2.1). W02 likewise says that technology helps identify and monitor resources but "is not a substitute for decision-making" (p.279). In Bangladesh, GPS trackers, mobile-phone news or drone surveillance could lower the cost of exclusion <span class="ev H">H</span>. That would not stop the resource being a CPR, but it could change which rights regime is sustainable.

### A subtle problem: the definitions themselves are shifting

Note this for yourself, because it recurs in later readings:

- **W02:** excludability and subtractability are **attributes of the resource**.
- **W09** (glossary, Box 1): excludability is the question of "who is and who is not a legitimate user", and subtractability "deals with the rules of resource distribution and allocation" <span class="ev D">D</span>. So Nayak and Berkes also use the two terms as **governance questions**.
- **W05:** defines "commons" as "resources used or governed by groups of heterogeneous users through agreed-upon institutional arrangements", a governance definition rather than a resource-attribute one.

None of these is wrong, but they are different <span class="ev I">I</span>. When an author says "excludability has declined", ask: has the physical attribute of the resource changed, or the institutional capacity to exclude?

::: misconceptions
<div class="wrong">"The haor is a commons, so there are no rules; anyone fishes as they like."</div>
<div class="right">Being a CPR does not mean having no rules. With no rules it is open access. Chilika had centuries-old caste-based rules (W06, p.135).</div>
<div class="wrong">"Common property means government property."</div>
<div class="right">In group property, rights are held by an identified group of users; government property is a separate regime (W02, Table 1).</div>
<div class="wrong">"Open access and common property are the same thing."</div>
<div class="right">W10 (§5.2) says Hardin's tragedy applies only to open access, and open access is historically short-lived: over time, rights become established.</div>
:::

# Rights, institutions and levels of rules [Making "fishers have rights" testable]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Question for this chapter:</b> why is "fishers have rights" so vague, and how can we break it into something testable?</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapter 1.</div>
<div><b>Used in:</b> W06 (p.135, p.142), W10 (§5.3–5.4), W02 (p.279)</div>
<div><b>Time:</b> 35 minutes</div>
</div>
:::

### Rights are a bundle, not a single thing

We often think of property as "owner or not". Commons scholarship splits it into five distinct rights:

- **access:** entering the area;
- **withdrawal:** taking fish;
- **management:** making harvesting rules;
- **exclusion:** deciding who may enter;
- **alienation:** selling or leasing the previous rights.

From this bundle come four kinds of right-holders: authorized user, claimant, proprietor and owner (Schlager & Ostrom 1992, cited in W10 §5.3) <span class="ev D">D</span>.

![[Figure 2.1]{.fnum} The bundle of rights: which holder has which right. The box below shows the Chilika paradox: holding legal rights and being able to exercise them are not the same.](../figures_en/fig_2_1_bundle_rights.svg){width=100%}

::: fig-read
From left to right the rights get "deeper": access is the shallowest, alienation the deepest. From top to bottom the holder's position strengthens. W10 notes that capture fisheries usually lack alienation rights, so fisher groups typically sit at the "claimant" or "proprietor" level.
:::

**Why the split is useful.** It turns "do they have rights?" into five testable questions. In the field you can ask:

1. Can you enter this beel?
2. Can you take fish?
3. Do you have a vote in the meeting that sets the mesh size?
4. Can you stop an outside boat?
5. Can you lease out your share?

W10 adds that proprietors and owners have incentives to invest for the long term, and that claimants who expect future returns may also have incentives to act as stewards <span class="ev D">D</span> (§5.3, p.64).

**The Chilika lesson.** Nayak and Berkes show that under the lease system, fisher villages held legal access and withdrawal rights, and de facto management, exclusion and alienation. By 2010, although still paying lease fees, they held "not a single right from the 'bundle'" in practice, while encroachers had moved from "thin access" to effectively full rights <span class="ev D">D</span> (W06, p.142). The authors therefore bring in Ribot and Peluso's (2003) "bundle of powers": a bundle of rights is not enough without the powers to exercise them.

### Institutions versus organizations

By "institution", commons scholarship means "regularized rules, norms and strategies that people use in making decisions" (Ostrom 1990, cited in W10 §5.4) <span class="ev D">D</span>; W09's glossary puts it briefly as "rules-in-use". An organization is a group of people or an office: a cooperative, the Department of Fisheries, FISHFED. Institutions are the rules of the game; organizations are the teams.

::: misconceptions
<div class="wrong">"Chilika has cooperatives, so its institutions are working."</div>
<div class="right">W06, Table 3: cooperatives "exist on paper, used for annual lease but otherwise either dormant or dysfunctional". Organizations can survive while effective rules collapse.</div>
:::

### Three levels of rules

Ostrom divides rules into levels:

- **operational** rules govern daily decisions: where, when and with what gear to fish;
- **collective-choice** rules set who changes the operational rules, and how;
- **constitutional** rules set who makes the collective-choice rules (after W10 §5.4).

![[Figure 2.2]{.fnum} Three levels of rules. The examples are invented for teaching, not data from any particular haor.](../figures_en/fig_2_2_rule_levels.svg){width=92%}

One of Ostrom's (2009) variables shows why the distinction matters: **GS6, collective-choice rules**. When users can make and enforce some of their own rules at the collective-choice level, the costs of transacting and of defending the resource against outsiders fall <span class="ev D">D</span> (W04, p.421). For Bangladesh's jalmohal (leased water-body) system the question becomes <span class="ev H">H</span>: can a lessee or an association make operational rules, or are all rules held at the constitutional level by the district administration or the ministry? This is worth asking at the workshop.

# Collective action, Hardin and design principles [When do self-interested people keep rules together?]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Question for this chapter:</b> when, and why, do people pursuing private gain follow shared rules?</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapters 1–2.</div>
<div><b>Used in:</b> W02 (p.279–281), W03 (Hardin and Maine sections), W09 (§4.1), W10 (§5.4)</div>
<div><b>Time:</b> 45 minutes</div>
</div>
:::

### What the dilemma is

A collective-action dilemma is a situation in which what is individually rational for each person produces a bad outcome for all. W09 (§4.1) explains it with the Prisoner's Dilemma: whatever the other player does, defection pays more in a single round, yet if both defect both do worse than if both had cooperated; in repeated play, cooperation can survive <span class="ev D">D</span>.

### Reconstructing Hardin: opening up the assumptions

Ostrom (2007) does something instructive: she writes out the five assumptions needed to turn Hardin's metaphor into a theory.

1. The resource system is a pasture.
2. No governance system is present.
3. Animals can be identified with their owners and sold.
4. Users are many relative to the size of the pasture.
5. Each user independently maximises short-term returns.

From these five assumptions comes the prediction of overharvesting <span class="ev D">D</span> (W03, "Conditions Leading to the Tragedy"). She concludes that Hardin's work rested on "an extremely sparse view of the commons".

![[Figure 3.1]{.fnum} Three systems on the same variable list. Hardin's assumptions hold almost exactly for roving bandits, so overharvesting occurred there; they do not hold for Maine's lobster fishers.](../figures_en/fig_3_1_hardin_matrix.svg){width=100%}

The next step is the most valuable for research design. In the laboratory, isolated and anonymous decisions repeatedly produce a "tragedy". But one small change brings participants close to the socially optimal harvest: letting them talk face to face between rounds, even though game theory predicts this should make no difference <span class="ev D">D</span> (W03). Here **one variable is changed** to test a theoretical assumption, one of the cleanest logical structures in science.

### What people are like: four behavioural types

W02 (p.279) sorts users into four types <span class="ev D">D</span>:

1. free-riders, who always act in narrow self-interest;
2. conditional cooperators, who cooperate only when assured they will not be exploited;
3. those who start cooperating in the hope that others will reciprocate;
4. perhaps a few genuine altruists.

Whether rules evolve depends on these proportions. Trust and reputation work better among people who know one another.

### When users organise themselves

Read together, W02 (p.281) and W04 make a general argument: self-organisation is likely when users expect the benefits of making rules to exceed the costs <span class="ev D">D</span>.

Benefits are higher when:

- the resource reliably yields something valuable;
- users depend on it for their livelihoods;
- they have some autonomy;
- they value the future.

Costs are higher when:

- the resource is large and complex;
- there is no shared understanding of how it behaves;
- interests diverge widely.

### Design principles: from eight to eleven

Ostrom (1990) found eight design principles in long-enduring commons institutions. Cox and colleagues (2010) reviewed 91 studies, found "strong support", and split three principles to make eleven <span class="ev D">D</span> (W09 §4.1; W10 Table 5.1):

| Principle | Short meaning | Haor question (invented example) |
|---|---|---|
| 1A User boundaries | Who is a legitimate user is clear | Fishers from which villages may fish this beel? |
| 1B Resource boundaries | The physical limits of the resource are clear | Where is the boundary when beel and haor merge in the monsoon? |
| 2A Congruence with local conditions | Rules fit local social and ecological conditions | Does the ban period match local spawning? |
| 2B Proportionality | Benefits proportional to labour or money contributed | Do those who dig the channel get a larger share? |
| 3 Collective choice | Those affected take part in changing rules | Who attends the meeting that sets the mesh size? |
| 4A Monitoring users | Monitors accountable to users watch harvesting | Who keeps watch at night? |
| 4B Monitoring the resource | The condition of the resource is tracked | Who records fish size and abundance? |
| 5 Graduated sanctions | Sanctions scale with the offence | A warning first, then a fine? |
| 6 Conflict resolution | Quick, cheap local forums | Does the village shalish work? |
| 7 Recognition of rights to organise | The state does not challenge local institutions | Does the district administration respect association rules? |
| 8 Nested enterprises | Activities organised in several layers | From village association to a haor-level federation? |

: Design principles (Cox et al. 2010 version, as in W10 Table 5.1). The third column's questions are invented for teaching.

::: {.box .caution}
<span class="label">Limits</span>
W10 (§5.4) gives two cautions. The principles must be used **probabilistically**, not as rules of certainty. And they deal mainly with **internal** factors, leaving out external drivers (population growth, technology, markets, enclosure). The research question of W06 grows exactly from this gap (Chapter 12).
:::

# The SES framework and going beyond panaceas [Managing complexity without a checklist]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Question for this chapter:</b> with hundreds of variables, can research accumulate at all? How does Ostrom handle the problem?</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapters 1–3.</div>
<div><b>Used in:</b> W03 (whole), W04 (whole), W10 (§5.5)</div>
<div><b>Time:</b> 45 minutes</div>
</div>
:::

### What a "panacea" is

A panacea is a cure-all: one solution imposed everywhere. W03's abstract says the special feature challenges the presumption that scholars can build simple predictive models of SESs and deduce universal solutions <span class="ev D">D</span>. W04 gives an example. On Canada's west coast, total allowable catch (TAC) quotas led to widespread dumping, misreporting and the closure of the groundfishery in 1995 <span class="ev D">D</span> (p.421). It was later reopened with more than 50 sectors, transferable quotas and neutral observers on every vessel.

### Framework, theory and model

The SES framework is a **classificatory framework**: a list and hierarchy of variables to look at. It does not itself say in which direction a variable acts; that is the job of theories and models <span class="ev I">I</span>. In W04's words, the framework provides "a common set of potentially relevant variables" for designing instruments, doing fieldwork and analysing findings (p.420).

![[Figure 4.1]{.fnum} Core subsystems of the SES framework. Simplified original redraw after Ostrom (2007 Fig.1; 2009 Fig.1), not a copy of the source figure. The feedback arrow is dashed because the framework says feedback exists but does not specify its path.](../figures_en/fig_4_1_ses_framework.svg){width=92%}

::: fig-read
Four core subsystems: the resource system (e.g. a coastal fishery), resource units (lobsters), users (fishers), and the governance system (rules and organizations). They are "relatively separable but interact" (W04, p.419); outcomes arise from their interactions and feed back to change them. Around them sit the wider social setting (S) and related ecosystems (ECO).
:::

### Managing complexity: partial decomposability

Agrawal (2002) identified more than 30 variables for sustainable commons and asked how knowledge could accumulate if so many had to be measured in every study (W03). Ostrom's answer is that SESs are "partially decomposable". In Herbert Simon's analogy, organisms are made of organs, organs of tissues, tissues of cells <span class="ev D">D</span>. This has three implications:

- variables can be arranged in classes and subclasses;
- subsystems function fairly separately, so a policy can be tried in one part;
- the whole is more than the sum of its parts, so the combination of variables A, B and C can behave differently from A, B and D.

For a biotechnologist the analogy is familiar. In a gene network, the effect of one gene often depends on the state of others (epistasis). The "interaction effects" Ostrom describes are the social-ecological form of the same logic <span class="ev I">I</span>.

### The ten variables

W04 applies the framework to one question: when will users invest time and effort to avoid a "tragedy"? It identifies ten second-tier variables that raise or lower the likelihood of self-organisation <span class="ev D">D</span> (p.420–421).

![[Figure 4.2]{.fnum} Qualitative shapes of the proposed effects of the ten variables. These depict W04's prose, not measured relationships. Size (RS3) and productivity (RS5) are hump-shaped, mobility (RU1) is negative, and number of users (U1) is conditional.](../figures_en/fig_4_2_ten_variables.svg){width=100%}

| Variable | W04's reasoning (brief) | Question for hilsa/haor <span class="ev H">H</span> |
|---|---|---|
| RS3 Size | Very large: boundaries and monitoring costly; very small: little benefit; moderate is most favourable | Haor-wide or beel-based governance? |
| RS5 Productivity | If exhausted or very abundant, no one organises for the future; some scarcity is needed | Does a sense of scarcity make bans acceptable? |
| RS7 Predictability | Users must be able to foresee the effects of rules; some fisheries approach chaos | Is uncertain flood timing an obstacle? |
| RU1 Mobility | Mobile units (wildlife, water in an unregulated river) make organising less likely | Hilsa is extremely mobile: how much can a local association do alone? |
| U1 Number of users | More users raise transaction costs, but big tasks need big groups | |
| U5 Leadership | More likely with respected leaders experienced in prior organisation; graduates and elders had a "strong positive effect" in 48 irrigation systems in Karnataka and Rajasthan | |
| U6 Norms / social capital | Reciprocity and trust lower transaction and monitoring costs | |
| U7 Knowledge of the SES | A shared understanding of how the resource behaves | Fishers' knowledge of spawning periods? |
| U8 Importance | When a large share of livelihood depends on the resource | |
| GS6 Collective-choice rules | Full autonomy to make own rules (Seri fishers, Nepal's forest user groups) | |

: Ostrom's (2009, pp.420–421) ten variables. The third column contains teaching questions, not claims from the source.

The author herself gives the biggest caution about the list: "the impact of any one variable depends on the values of other SES variables", and the variables "interact in a nonlinear fashion" <span class="ev D">D</span> (p.421). The list is **not a checklist** from which outcomes can be predicted by ticking boxes.

### Maine lobster: why a rule worked

W03 presents a subtle case <span class="ev D">D</span>. Maine's experience ran in four steps:

1. **1870s law fails.** Maine banned harvesting egg-bearing female lobsters, but the law did not work: fishers scrubbed off the eggs and sold the lobsters.
2. **The state adds a mark.** It began punching holes in the tails of egg-bearing females.
3. **1948: the V-notch law.** Selling a lobster marked with a "V-notch" became illegal; the notch lasts two or three moults.
4. **Fishers adopt the norm.** Fishers began voluntarily V-notching egg-bearing lobsters and refrained from selling lobsters notched by others.

The result was a reliable, easily seen signal that sustains reciprocity.

The logic of W03's Fig. 2: the rule would fail if lobsters could not be returned to the sea (RU2), if most lobsters caught at one harbour migrated to distant harbours (RU1), or if the notch disappeared quickly (RU6) <span class="ev D">D</span>. W04 makes the same point: eggs are visible on a lobster's abdomen, but a fish's eggs are internal, so a ban on taking "gravid fish" is hard to monitor (p.421).

::: {.box .ecology}
<span class="label">Hilsa connection</span>
A gravid hilsa's eggs are inside its body, so a rule like "no catching egg-bearing hilsa" cannot be checked at the moment of capture, as it can for lobster <span class="ev I">I</span>. Time-based and area-based bans are probably more workable for this reason: what is monitored is no longer "the condition of the fish" but "is a net in the water?". This is a reasonable interpretation; whether Bangladeshi policymakers actually decided on this reasoning has not been checked here <span class="ev U">U</span>.
:::

### Roving bandits versus "harbour gangs"

Berkes and colleagues (2006) use "roving bandits" for fishing fleets that deplete valuable coastal species and move on to other regions; new markets appear so fast that local institutions cannot respond before the resource is gone (cited in W03). Ostrom shows that here Hardin's five assumptions hold almost exactly, so the prediction came true. On the Maine coast in the 1980s, outsiders depleted green sea urchins for export, while on the same coast lobster fishers had lived for generations and built norms of leadership and trust <span class="ev D">D</span>. Ostrom's conclusion: the key to turning roving bandits into organised local groups is to shift harvesters' time horizon from short to long term.

# Commons as process: commonisation and decommonisation [Seeing change over time]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Question for this chapter:</b> what new things become visible when the commons is treated as an ongoing process rather than a fixed regime, and what risks does that create?</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapters 1–4.</div>
<div><b>Used in:</b> W06, W07, W08, W09, W10 §5.6; the workshop title</div>
<div><b>Time:</b> 40 minutes</div>
</div>
:::

### Why the "process" concept arrived

Agrawal and colleagues (2023, §2.2.2) identify a tendency in commons research. Scholars have paid more attention to **stability** (the persistence of institutions and resources) than to change and dynamics, because since Ostrom many aimed to show that common property can protect resources <span class="ev D">D</span>. Nayak and Berkes (2011) had turned this gap into a research question about a decade earlier. Their article opens: "Property rights to resources are dynamic." <span class="ev D">D</span> (W06, p.132).

### Definitions

**Commonisation**: "a process through which a resource gets converted into a jointly used resource under commons institutions that deal with excludability and subtractability". **Decommonisation**: "a process through which a jointly used resource under commons institutions loses these essential characteristics" <span class="ev D">D</span> (W06, p.132). The two processes are "continuous and potentially two-way" (p.133).

By 2022 the definition shifts slightly: commonisation is "a process through which a resource is converted into a joint-use regime under commons institutions **and collective action**" (W09, abstract). Collective action is now part of the definition. W10 (§5.6) combines the two: "commons institutions and collective action that deal with excludability and subtractability". Notice these small changes; this is how a concept matures <span class="ev I">I</span>.

![[Figure 5.1]{.fnum} The commonisation–decommonisation continuum. Original redraw after Nayak & Berkes (2011 Fig.1; 2022 Fig.1) and Berkes et al. (W10 Fig.5.2). "Favourable/unfavourable" is the authors' own declared value position (W09 §6).](../figures_en/fig_5_1_continuum.svg){width=100%}

::: fig-read
The current state (central dot) can move either way. The direction depends on drivers at many levels, from local to global. The middle box is the most interesting: commonisation in some elements of a commons and decommonisation in others can run at the same time.
:::

### Three possible trajectories

W09 (§3) gives three possibilities <span class="ev D">D</span>:

1. commonisation under favourable conditions;
2. decommonisation under unfavourable conditions;
3. both at once, as in Mexico's Comcáac (Seri) fisheries.

The authors add that each, on its own, is a "unidirectional evolutionary process". When the two are linked by mutual feedback, they may form a "co-evolutionary process".

### "Favourable" for whom?

W09's conclusion contains an honest admission that is easy to miss. The distinction between "favourable" and "unfavourable" is "a normative position that reflects our preference for local social and economic controls". An analyst aligned with large companies might see privatisation as the "favourable" direction, and the authors say this comparison is beyond their scope <span class="ev D">D</span> (§6). As a scientist, keep two questions apart:

1. Under what conditions does change go in which direction? This is an empirical question.
2. Which direction is desirable? This is a question of values.

The model assumes an answer to the second question. It is comparatively less specific about the first <span class="ev I">I</span>.

### "Ephemeral commons": what we know and what we don't

The workshop title includes "Ephemeral Commons of Bangladesh". The assigned readings contain one explicit use of the term. W09 (§5) writes that Singh "reports that in certain contexts, the very resource around which commonisation and decommonisation processes revolve often remains elusive, leading to the suggestion that they are ephemeral commons" <span class="ev D">D</span>. The work cited is Chapter 4 of W08: Vipul Singh, "Governing Fluvial Commons in Colonial Bihar: Alluvion and Diluvion Regulation and Decommonisation" (verified in the publisher's contents). Alluvion is new land built by river silt; diluvion is land lost to erosion. In this sense "ephemeral" means **the resource itself comes and goes**.

::: {.box .caution}
<span class="label">What this book does not know</span>
W08's chapter was not read for this edition <span class="ev U">U</span>. No assigned reading defines the sense in which the workshop organisers use "ephemeral" (hydrological-temporal, institutional, or both). The framework below is therefore our proposed analytical scheme, not a claim from the sources.
:::

In Bangladesh's wetlands, "ephemeral" can be tested in at least three distinct senses <span class="ev H">H</span>:

| Dimension | What comes and goes | Testable question | Data needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrological-temporal | In the monsoon, haor, beels and fields merge into one water; in the dry season they separate | Do right-holders at the same place change between wet and dry seasons? | Seasonal access maps, interviews, remote-sensed water extent |
| Geomorphological | Chars rise and erode (alluvion/diluvion) | How are rights over new land established or lost? | Historical survey records, satellite time series |
| Institutional | Lease cycles, ban seasons, project lifetimes | Do rules match the rhythm of the resource (design principle 2A)? | Lease records, ban dates vs water levels and spawning season |

: Proposed scheme for analysing "ephemeral commons" (hypothetical, for checking at the workshop).

The value of this scheme is a reminder. If the resource itself is ephemeral, meeting design principle 1B (clear resource boundaries) is hard, and Ostrom's (2009) RS7 (predictability) turns unfavourable <span class="ev I">I</span>. That makes a strong workshop question: in Bangladesh's ephemeral commons, do time-based rules (when, for how long) work better than boundary-based design principles?
