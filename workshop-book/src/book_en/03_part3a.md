### What could be verified

The book's chapters (archive.org catalogue) <span class="ev D">D</span>:

1. Reflections on the commons
2. An institutional approach to the study of self-organization and self-governance in CPR situations
3. Analyzing long-enduring, self-organized, and self-governed CPRs
4. Analyzing institutional change
5. Analyzing institutional failures and fragilities
6. A framework for analysis of self-organizing and self-governing CPRs

### How other readings use the book

What this book knows about it is indirect, so it is stated with care. W05 says Ostrom sought a "parsimonious" set of design principles for the commons, modelled on the short lists of conditions in market theory, the Weberian ideal type of bureaucracy, and Arrow's work on voting <span class="ev D">D</span> (§1). W10 says the principles "draw mainly from community-based examples and from longstanding commons institutions" <span class="ev D">D</span> (§5.4). W09 gives an adapted list (Box 2). The book's central method is thus **inductive comparison of long-enduring successful cases**, followed by testing against cases of change and failure <span class="ev I">I</span>; the titles of Chapters 4 and 5 point to that second step.

### Reading priorities

| Order | Chapter | Why | What to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | Source of the design principles | Which cases were selected as long-enduring, and how "success" is defined |
| 2 | 1 | Critique of Hardin, the Prisoner's Dilemma and Olson | Which assumptions Ostrom challenges (compare with Chapter 3 of this book) |
| 3 | 5 | Failure and fragility | Does the absence of design principles explain failure? How much do external drivers appear? |
| 4 | 4 | Institutional change | Any forerunner of W06's "process" concept? |
| 5 | 2, 6 | Theoretical framework | Compare later with W03–W04 |

: Suggested order for reading Ostrom (1990).

::: {.box .caution}
<span class="label">A methodological question to keep in mind</span>
Deriving "conditions of success" from successful cases risks **selection on the dependent variable**: if failed cases share the same features, those features are not conditions of success. While reading, check how far Ostrom's chapter on failure (5) handles this risk. Cox et al.'s (2010) re-evaluation of 91 studies (W10 §5.4) is a later answer to it.
:::

# W02 · Ostrom, Burger, Field, Norgaard & Policansky (1999) [Revisiting the Commons: Local Lessons, Global Challenges]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Publication:</b> *Science* 284(5412):278–282. doi:10.1126/science.284.5412.278</div>
<div><b>Genre:</b> Science Compass review; no new primary data.</div>
<div><b>Coverage:</b> <span class="ev D">full text</span> 6-page PDF (utexas.edu copy), including the captions of Tables 1–2 and Figure 1.</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapters 1 and 3.</div>
</div>
:::

### Gentle entry

Thirty years after Hardin's article, five researchers from different institutions wrote a reassessment. The article lists their affiliations as Indiana University's institutions research centre, Rutgers' Environmental and Occupational Health Sciences Institute, the Carnegie Institution, UC Berkeley's Energy and Resources Group, and the US National Research Council. The core message: tragedies are real but not inevitable; local experience teaches lessons, but global commons (climate, international river basins, large marine ecosystems) bring new problems. The article's most quoted sentence: "Institutional diversity may be as important as biological diversity for our long-term survival" (p.278) <span class="ev D">D</span>.

### Reasoning reconstruction

1. **Problem** <span class="ev D">D</span>: Hardin's "starkness" became a rationale for central control and portrayed users as helpless (p.278).
2. **First evidence** <span class="ev D">D</span>: satellite imagery of Inner Asian grasslands (citing Sneath 1998).
    - Degraded: about three-quarters of the Russian section, over one-third of the Chinese section, about one-tenth of the Mongolian section.
    - Mongolia kept traditional group property with seasonal movement; Russia and China imposed state collectives, and China later moved to household allocations.
    - Conclusion: "socialism and privatization are both associated with more degradation" (p.278).
3. **Conceptual clarification** <span class="ev D">D</span>: the CPR definition; resource attributes and rights kept apart; a list of resource characteristics (p.279).
4. **Institutional argument** <span class="ev D">D</span>: two elements are needed together, restricting access and creating incentives to invest. North Pacific halibut: catch limits protected the stock, but because access was not limited, the "race" brought waste, danger and poor-quality fish (p.279).
5. **Behavioural argument** <span class="ev D">D</span>: the selfish model fits competitive markets but not settings where people can communicate, sanction and make rules; four behavioural types (Chapter 3).
6. **Second evidence** <span class="ev D">D</span>: irrigation in Nepal (Figure 8.1).
7. **Generalisation** <span class="ev I">I</span>: a list of conditions for local success; the wider state context can help or hinder.
8. **Future** <span class="ev D">D</span> (p.281–282): six challenges of global commons:
    - scaling up;
    - cultural diversity;
    - interlinked CPRs;
    - accelerating change;
    - the unanimity rule in treaties;
    - "only one globe with which to experiment".

![[Figure 8.1]{.fnum} Ownership and cropping intensity in Nepal's irrigation systems. Values verbatim from Ostrom et al. (1999) Table 2 (original source Lam 1998, p.106). Chart original; bar heights proportional to values.](../figures_en/fig_8_1_nepal_irrigation.svg){width=92%}

### Claims and exhibits: what the Nepal table shows

Table 2 shows cropping intensities of 246% at the head end and 237% at the tail end in 97 farmer-owned systems, against 208% and 182% in 21 government systems <span class="ev D">D</span>. This is the second rung of the inference ladder: **association**.

The authors add a regression result. Controlling for system size, terrain slope, income variation and alternative water sources, government ownership and modern headworks still have a negative effect on water delivered to the tail end (p.280). The control variables move the claim somewhat from association towards causation, but one question remains <span class="ev I">I</span>. Did the government build systems at random, or in places that were already difficult? If the latter, selection bias could explain part of the association. The answer cannot be known without reading the original study (Lam 1998) <span class="ev U">U</span>.

One small observation. In the table, the tail-end difference is larger (F=20.33), yet P=0.004 is bigger than the head-end P=0.002. With the same sample size, a larger F normally gives a smaller P, so the figures are worth checking against the original source <span class="ev I">I</span>. This does not change the direction of the claim, but it teaches checking quoted numbers before copying them.

### "Why together" analysis

| Component A | Component B | A alone | B alone | Together | If B removed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grasslands (Russia–China–Mongolia) | Nepal irrigation | State and market can both fail | State engineering can do worse than farmer rules | Same direction in two resource sectors: a stronger case for generalising | Claim limited to one landscape |
| Local lessons | Global challenges | Commons can work | Global commons are hard | Which lessons transfer and which do not | The article would be one-sidedly optimistic |

: Two important pairings in W02.

### Writing structure (reverse outline)

- **Paragraph 1:** presents Hardin respectfully.
- **Paragraph 2:** shows the political consequences of his influence.
- **Paragraph 3:** brings in hard evidence (the satellite image) straight away.

The job of this order <span class="ev I">I</span> is to show the reader at once that the question is empirical, not merely theoretical. Then come the definitions, because the reader now wants to know what exactly "commons" means. The article moves from local to global at the end, which makes it relevant to *Science*'s broad readership. A lesson for your own papers: a short, hard piece of evidence before the definitions holds the reader's attention.

### Critique

- **Strengths:** a multidisciplinary author team; a clear separation of resource attributes and rights; failures of both state and market shown.
- **Limits:** the evidence is indirect and selected. In the grassland comparison, climate, population and history also differ between countries, so the rights regime cannot be called the only cause <span class="ev I">I</span>. The article itself uses "associated with", not causal language. Power and distribution appear (the "distributional consequences" of exclusion, p.280) but are not central; W05 later names this gap.

### Questions for the author (W02, if a speaker is confirmed)

1. You equate "institutional diversity" with biological diversity. How would you measure institutional diversity so that the claim becomes testable? *Why:* the sentence is widely quoted, but the article offers no measure. *Follow-up:* has Bangladesh's jalmohal policy reduced that diversity?
2. How has your view of the distributional consequences of exclusion (p.280) changed since 1999?

### Scientist's decision lab

**Problem:** you want to show that rights regimes affect grassland degradation, and you have satellite images of the border regions of three countries. Which design would you choose, and which alternative explanations would you want to rule out? *(Guided answer in Chapter 19.)*

# W03 · Ostrom (2007), *A diagnostic approach for going beyond panaceas* [PNAS Special Feature]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Publication:</b> *PNAS* 104(39):15181–15187. doi:10.1073/pnas.0702288104 (PMC2000497)</div>
<div><b>Genre:</b> conceptual-methodological article with examples; no new primary data.</div>
<div><b>Coverage:</b> <span class="ev D">full text</span> PMC HTML; Fig.1, Fig.2, Table 1 and Table 2 were images and were not viewed directly; their content is known from the text and from W04 Table 1.</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapters 3–4.</div>
</div>
:::

### Gentle entry

A doctor diagnoses before prescribing. Ostrom argues that in social-ecological problems, policymakers often prescribe without diagnosis: privatisation everywhere, or nationalisation everywhere, or community-based management everywhere. The article's aim is the first step towards a "diagnostic method". It arranges variables in tiers, so that knowledge about which combinations produce which outcomes can accumulate.

### Reasoning reconstruction

1. **Problem** <span class="ev D">D</span>: the attraction of simple solutions persists. Holling et al. are quoted: the problems are "non-linear ... cross-scale ... evolutionary", and natural and social systems "are one system".
2. **Metaphor** <span class="ev D">D</span>: a "rugged landscape". Optimising a few variables risks getting stuck on a low hill.
3. **Obstacle** <span class="ev D">D</span>: Agrawal found more than 30 variables, and measuring all of them in every study is impossible.
4. **Solution** <span class="ev D">D</span>: partial decomposability and a multitier framework; the question decides how deep to go.
5. **Demonstration 1** <span class="ev D">D</span>: Hardin rewritten in the framework's language (five assumptions), showing that the framework makes assumptions explicit.
6. **Demonstration 2** <span class="ev D">D</span>: roving bandits (assumptions true, so tragedy) versus Maine lobster (assumptions false, so recovery).
7. **Methodological claim** <span class="ev D">D</span>: the framework will reduce "method wars". Modellers, case-study researchers and large-N statisticians all work at different tiers of one framework.
8. **Conclusion** <span class="ev D">D</span>: "Identifying a clear question must always be the first step".

### Decision audit: why rewrite Hardin?

- **Immediate why:** retelling a familiar story in new language lets readers see the framework at work.
- **Structural why:** critics could have said Ostrom was calling Hardin "wrong". The rewriting shows that Hardin's model is right for **a particular kind of system**, and that the kind can be recognised by the framework's variables.
- **Counterfactual why:** without this section, the framework would remain an abstract list whose practical value readers would have to take on trust <span class="ev I">I</span>.

### "Why together": roving bandits and Maine lobster

The two examples are on the same coast, under the same state. Green urchins were depleted; lobster survived. The condition of autonomy held in both cases, but the urchin harvesters were not local (U2), did not share norms (U6), and harvested quickly for export (S5) <span class="ev D">D</span>. The pairing works much like a controlled comparison: the geographic and legal setting is nearly constant while user characteristics differ <span class="ev I">I</span>. But the biology of the species also differs (lobsters can be returned and marked), so two clusters of variables changed at once. Ostrom herself separates the biological conditions in Fig. 2.

### Writing structure

The article opens with the question "What Can Be Done?", a natural next step after the special feature's introductory article on panaceas. In the middle, two examples bring the theory down to earth. The closing section on method wars addresses each audience separately: modellers, case-study researchers and statisticians <span class="ev I">I</span>. A lesson for you: in an article proposing a framework, include at least one "old problem in new language" example.

### Critique

- **Strengths:** a tool for making assumptions explicit; an argument for treating methods as complementary rather than opposed.
- **Weaknesses:**
    - The framework does not specify the direction of variables or causal paths; the author herself admits "A major challenge is defining all variables" <span class="ev D">D</span>. Hence the risk of using it as a checklist.
    - The examples come mainly from successful cases.
    - Power and politics appear as variables (S, GS) but are not central to the analysis <span class="ev I">I</span>.

# W04 · Ostrom (2009), *A General Framework for Analyzing Sustainability of Social-Ecological Systems* [Science Perspective]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Publication:</b> *Science* 325(5939):419–422. doi:10.1126/science.1172133</div>
<div><b>Genre:</b> Perspective (special section on complex systems and networks).</div>
<div><b>Coverage:</b> <span class="ev D">full text</span> 5-page PDF. The manifest's warning is confirmed: the first PDF page carries the end of the preceding article (ecological networks, doi:10.1126/science.1170749) and the last pages the start of the next one, "Economic Networks: The New Challenges". The target article is pp.419–422 only.</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapter 4, W03.</div>
</div>
:::

### Gentle entry and relation to W03

W04 is essentially an updated, condensed version of W03's framework, written for a broad science readership. Table 1 is "Adapted from (12)", and reference 12 is W03 <span class="ev D">D</span>. What is new is the application of the framework to a specific question: the ten variables (Chapter 4). The first paragraph's analogy is biological: an SES is "analogous to organisms composed of organs, organs of tissues..." (p.419).

### Reasoning reconstruction

1. **Concern** <span class="ev D">D</span>: the risk of losing fisheries, forests and water; disciplines use different languages, so "isolated knowledge does not cumulate".
2. **Error of earlier theory** <span class="ev D">D</span>: it assumed users would never organise; research showed some government policies accelerated destruction.
3. **Conditional truth** <span class="ev D">D</span>: the prediction of collapse holds in "very large, highly valuable, open-access systems when the resource harvesters are diverse, do not communicate, and fail to develop rules".
4. **Framework** <span class="ev D">D</span>: four core subsystems plus I, O, S, ECO.
5. **Applied question** <span class="ev D">D</span>: when will users invest time and effort?
6. **Theoretical answer** <span class="ev D">D</span>: when expected benefits exceed perceived costs; but "Accurate and reliable measures of users' perceived benefits and costs are difficult and costly to obtain".
7. **Ten variables** <span class="ev D">D</span> and their rationale.
8. **Cautions** <span class="ev D">D</span>:
    - variables interact non-linearly;
    - initial rules fail if they do not fit local conditions;
    - colonial powers disregarded local institutions.
9. **Future** <span class="ev D">D</span>: comparable databases; both quantitative and qualitative data are needed.

### Decision audit: why the theoretical answer is not tested directly

The cost-benefit theory is reasonable, but because "perceived" benefits and costs are hard to measure, the author uses **proxy variables** instead of a direct test <span class="ev I">I</span>. Size, productivity, leadership and so on are argued to influence benefits and costs. This is a deliberate design choice: capturing an unobservable concept through observable variables. The risk is that the link between proxy and outcome can arise by other routes. "Leadership", for instance, may itself be a product of earlier successful organisation (reverse causation).

### Critique

- The ten variables are said to be "frequently identified", but the article does not explain by what criterion these ten were chosen <span class="ev U">U</span>.
- In explaining U1, the author herself shows that the effect can run either way. That is honest, and it shows why a variable list alone does not predict.
- The fisheries examples are from coastal seas. In seasonal floodplain fisheries, RS2 (clarity of boundaries) and RS7 may behave differently <span class="ev H">H</span>.

# W05 · Agrawal, Erbaugh & Pradhan (2023), *The Commons* [Annual Review of Environment and Resources]{.en}

::: chapter-meta
<div><b>Publication:</b> *Annu. Rev. Environ. Resour.* 48:531–558. doi:10.1146/annurev-environ-112320-113509 (CC BY 4.0)</div>
<div><b>Genre:</b> review article.</div>
<div><b>Coverage:</b> <span class="ev D">main text complete</span> Annual Reviews HTML; Figure 1 image not viewed directly; reference list not read in full.</div>
<div><b>Prerequisites:</b> Chapters 1–4, 6.</div>
</div>
:::

### Gentle entry

This is a "map of the field": what commons research has achieved, how it has changed, and where it should go. The authors claim it is "one of the more successful research programs in the social and ecological sciences" <span class="ev D">D</span>. This is a self-assessment by researchers inside the field; keep that in mind as you read <span class="ev I">I</span>.

### Structure and argument

1. **Three lasting contributions** <span class="ev D">D</span> (§1): clear conceptual language; self-organised institutions can substitute for markets and states; commons are ubiquitous and durable, not a historical curiosity.
2. **From parsimony to complexity** <span class="ev D">D</span>: design principles → critique (politics, culture, justice) → SES framework. Accumulating knowledge has therefore become harder, and consistency in measurement and terminology is needed.
3. **Theoretical contributions** <span class="ev D">D</span> (§2.1):
    - types of goods;
    - repeated interaction and reputation;
    - from institutions to outcomes;
    - communication;
    - power, gender, Indigenous management, culture.
4. **Sectoral contributions** <span class="ev D">D</span> (§2.2): forests, grazing, irrigation, fisheries, wildlife; and three self-criticisms.
5. **Methods** <span class="ev D">D</span> (§2.3): observation, simulation, games, experiments.
6. **Three eras** <span class="ev D">D</span> (§3.1): before 2000, 2000–2010, after 2010.
7. **Future** <span class="ev D">D</span> (§3.2): power, equity and justice; commoning; big data and causal inference.

### The most important passage for this workshop

The three tendencies in §2.2.2 almost directly justify the work of W06–W09 <span class="ev I">I</span>:

1. Research is confined to single resource types, with little comparison across them.
2. **Attention to stability rather than change.** Researchers wanted to show that common property works, so less attention went to transforming arrangements that are unjust.
3. Little attention to how institutions change people's preferences, identities and subjectivities <span class="ev D">D</span>.

W06's "Maa Chilika" and "disconnection" speak to the third tendency.

### Two conceptual cautions

**Definition.** In the first sentence, commons are "resources used or governed by groups of heterogeneous users through agreed-upon institutional arrangements". This differs from W02's resource-attribute definition of a CPR: here, the institutional arrangement is part of the definition <span class="ev I">I</span>. Under this definition it is unclear whether open access counts as a "commons" at all.

**Commoning.** W05 defines commoning as "shared, collaborative, and situated social practices through which groups of people with joint goals create the commons" <span class="ev D">D</span> (§3.2.2). It acknowledges that this literature takes "a different path": rather than searching for conditions, it seeks to remake social and political relations, often from an anti-capitalist stance. This mixes descriptive science with a value-driven project; keep the two apart while reading <span class="ev I">I</span>.

### The methods section: a quick reading guide

W05's methods section complements Chapter 6. Remember two points in particular:

- From 1990 to 2004, only 36 of 172 observational studies had more than 30 observations <span class="ev D">D</span>.
- Coordinated experiments apply the same intervention at random in several countries to reduce problems of external validity: forests in Liberia, Peru and Uganda; water in Brazil, Costa Rica and China <span class="ev D">D</span>.

W05 summarises these experiments as showing that externally initiated community monitoring improved sustainable use. The size of the effects, and whether they were consistent across countries, has not been checked in this book against the original publication <span class="ev U">U</span>.

### Critique

- **Strengths:** broad, recent and method-aware; candid about the field's weaknesses.
- **Limits:** comparatively little on fisheries; nothing on Bangladesh or South Asian floodplains; no "ephemeral commons". The concluding language is hopeful and idealistic ("hope", "dignity"), and should be read separately from the review's descriptive sections <span class="ev I">I</span>.
